Sustainable Packaging Attitudes Study — Target Audience Brief
Meridian Insights Group · Q1 2026

Prepared by: J. Reyes, Strategy Associate
For: Internal team review, March 2026

The audience we're working with for this study is the middle-to-upper-middle
US household — income band $75,000 to $150,000 annually — and what we're
really trying to understand is how these people think about packaging when
they're actually shopping, not when they're being surveyed about their
values. Because those are two very different conversations, and the study
makes that really clear.

We pulled n=1,247 respondents, fielded from January 15 to February 28, mixed
mode, plus twenty-four in-depth interviews for depth. The overall finding
that jumped out at me was that sixty-seven percent say packaging
sustainability genuinely influences their purchases, but fifty-eight percent
distrust the claims they see on pack. That tension basically defines the
whole category. Consumers want this to matter. They don't believe most of
what brands are telling them about it. So the work is less about convincing
them that sustainability matters and more about earning the right to be
believed when you say you're doing it.

There are four segments that came out of the latent class analysis and
they're pretty distinct. About twenty-two percent are what we're calling
Conviction Buyers — these are the people who are genuinely sustainability-
driven, skew female, skew 28 to 42, higher-income, living in coastal metros
mostly. They pay a premium, they switch brands, they punish greenwashing
aggressively, and they get their information from social media creators who
call out brands for lying. They're the smallest segment but they're loud and
they shape category norms. Seventy-three percent of this group has paid a
sustainability premium in the last year.

Then we have Pragmatic Parents at thirty-four percent, which is the biggest
chunk. Families with kids, mixed gender, 32 to 48, household size of four or
five on average, income $75k to $125k mostly. These are the people where
sustainability is a real consideration but it's competing with budget,
availability, and honestly just the exhaustion of keeping a family running.
They shop at warehouse clubs and supercenters, they learn about products
from retailer apps and from their friends, and they'll pick the sustainable
option at price parity — but not at a ten percent premium. This is where the
volume lives.

Twenty-eight percent are Skeptical Shoppers, which I find the most
interesting segment honestly. They're older, mean age 54, lean male, and
they're not anti-sustainability at all — they're just absolutely done being
marketed to. Their trust in institutions score is the lowest of any segment,
2.1 on a 5-point scale. They want certification numbers, they want
third-party auditors, they want specificity. They read labels carefully and
they punish vagueness. You can win with them, but you have to earn it with
evidence. Sixty-eight percent of them recognize the FSC logo unprompted.

And then sixteen percent are Convenience Prioritizers, the hardest segment
to move. They're younger, urban, delivery-first, and packaging sustainability
basically doesn't register for them as a decision factor. Only twelve percent
have paid a premium. Persuasion isn't going to work here. The move with this
group is product design — make the sustainable option the default in
delivery channels and let it ride without any claim-based messaging. Don't
spend ad dollars here.

The big messaging implication that kept coming up across all the segments
except Conviction Buyers is that generic green language actively hurts
credibility. The words "green," "eco-friendly," and "natural" without
supporting certification drove statistically significant credibility
declines. "100% sustainable" scored lowest of anything we tested. So the
creative brief should basically read: if you can't back it up with a number
and a year and a third party, don't say it.

Willingness-to-pay averages 8.3% across the whole sample but that's a
misleading number because the variance between segments is huge — 17.4% for
Conviction, 1.8% for Convenience. Pricing strategy has to be segment-aware.

Next steps I'd recommend: a follow-up qual with Pragmatic Parents specifically
around price parity thresholds, and a claim-testing quant wave with the
Skeptical segment to figure out exactly which certification logos move the
needle.
