# Sustainable Packaging Attitudes Study
**Meridian Insights Group · Research Report · Q1 2026**

*Study ID: MIG-SP-2026-Q1 · Field dates: January 15 – February 28, 2026*
*Sample: n=1,247 US households, annual income $75,000–$150,000*
*Report prepared: March 14, 2026*

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Sustainable packaging has crossed the threshold from niche preference to mainstream purchase driver among middle- and upper-middle-income US households. **67% of respondents** report that packaging sustainability meaningfully influences their purchase decisions, and **41% have switched brands in the past 12 months** citing packaging concerns as a primary or contributing factor. However, the category is defined as much by skepticism as enthusiasm: **58% of respondents distrust generic "sustainable" labeling claims**, and unprompted recall of third-party certifications is low (average 1.4 named per respondent).

Average willingness-to-pay premium for verified sustainable packaging is **8.3%**, but this figure masks profound segment-level variance: **73% of Conviction Buyers** will pay a premium compared to just **12% of Convenience Prioritizers**. This divergence, not the aggregate number, should drive packaging strategy.

We identify four attitudinal segments that together explain the purchase behavior observed in the sample. Each segment exhibits distinct information-sourcing habits, trust profiles, and price elasticities. The balance of this report details those segments, the behaviors that distinguish them, and the messaging implications for brands operating in CPG, food & beverage, household goods, and direct-to-consumer categories.

---

## 2. Methodology

**Mixed-mode design.** The study combined a quantitative online survey (n=1,247) with 24 in-depth interviews (IDIs) conducted remotely via video. Online recruitment used a dual-source panel (Prodege + NORC AmeriSpeak). IDI subjects were drawn from survey respondents who opted in for follow-up; IDI sampling was stratified to recruit at least 5 respondents per identified segment.

**Screening criteria.** US residents, 25–65, reporting household annual income between $75,000 and $150,000, with primary or shared responsibility for household grocery and household-goods purchasing decisions. The 14-item screener (see `screener.qsf`) excluded respondents employed in market research, packaging, or communications/PR roles.

**Fielding.** The survey instrument averaged 17 minutes to complete. Response rate was 24.3% among panel invitees; data were weighted to Census CPS benchmarks on `age_range`, `gender_distribution`, and `geographic_region`.

**Segmentation method.** Latent class analysis on 31 attitudinal and behavioral indicators, four-class solution selected on BIC and interpretability. Class assignment probabilities averaged 0.81 (min 0.66).

## 3. Segments Discovered

### 3.1 Conviction Buyers — 22% of sample (n=274)

**Segment ID: `conviction_buyers`**

Conviction Buyers are the ideological core of the sustainable packaging market. They rank `environmental_concern` at a mean of 4.6 of 5, the highest of any segment, and exhibit low `price_sensitivity` when sustainability claims are present and credible. Demographically, they skew female (58%), aged 28–42, concentrated in the Northeast and West regions, with households of 2–3. Income is higher-skew — 61% report $100k–$150k.

Their `information_sources` are dominated by social media platforms (particularly Instagram and TikTok creators focused on zero-waste lifestyles) and brand websites with transparent sourcing disclosures. NGO reports and certification body websites (B Corp, FSC, How2Recycle) figure prominently in unprompted recall. `brand_affinity` runs high; Conviction Buyers name an average of 3.8 preferred sustainable brands unprompted versus 1.1 for the overall sample.

**Headline behaviors.** 73% have paid a premium of 10% or more for sustainable packaging in the past year. 51% report boycotting a brand over packaging concerns in the past 12 months. `purchase_frequency` of explicitly marketed sustainable products is weekly (64%) or monthly (29%).

> *"I check the label before anything else. If a brand isn't putting their certifications on the front, I assume they don't have any."* — R-0007, 36F, South

### 3.2 Pragmatic Parents — 34% of sample (n=424)

**Segment ID: `pragmatic_parents`**

The largest segment. Pragmatic Parents are households with children (average household_size 4.2) where sustainability is one consideration among several, not the organizing principle. Mean `environmental_concern` is 3.8; the segment believes sustainability matters but subordinates it to family budget, product safety, and availability. `health_consciousness` runs high (4.1 mean) — partly overlapping with packaging concerns via BPA, PFAS, and microplastic anxieties.

They report moderate `price_sensitivity` and are the segment most responsive to price-parity sustainable options. **45% have paid a premium for sustainable packaging**, but almost always a small one (5% or less). Their `information_sources` emphasize retailer websites (Target, Costco, Walmart apps), friends and family recommendations, and parenting-focused digital content. Social media plays a smaller role than for Conviction Buyers.

**Headline behaviors.** 52% report "often" checking recyclability labels when shopping. 38% have switched brands in the past 12 months citing packaging, usually in favor of a direct competitor at similar price rather than a premium sustainable alternative. `channel_preference` favors large-format retailers (warehouse clubs, supercenters) and grocery delivery.

> *"If the sustainable version is within a dollar or two, I'll grab it. But I've got four kids and a budget, so I'm not going to double my grocery bill for a principle."* — R-0044, 35F, South

### 3.3 Skeptical Shoppers — 28% of sample (n=349)

**Segment ID: `skeptical_shoppers`**

Skeptical Shoppers are older (mean age 54), lean male (53%), and present the clearest barrier to generic sustainability marketing. Their `trust_in_institutions` score is the lowest of any segment at 2.1 — not because they oppose sustainability on its merits, but because they believe most "sustainable" claims are marketing rather than substantive. **72% of Skeptical Shoppers agreed** with the statement *"Most companies use sustainability claims to justify higher prices without changing their practices."*

Their `information_sources` are dominated by news articles (particularly consumer-affairs coverage), detailed product labels, and Consumer Reports-style long-form reviews. They read labels carefully, distrust front-of-pack marketing, and respond to specific third-party certifications more than any other segment — **68% recognize the FSC logo unprompted** versus 43% of the overall sample.

**Headline behaviors.** 32% have paid a premium for sustainable packaging, but always conditional on a credible certification. 29% have returned a product in the past year over packaging complaints, the highest rate of any segment. `decision_making_style` is deliberative; they research before switching and rarely revert.

> *"Put the certification number on it. Tell me who audited you and when. Anybody can slap a green leaf on a box."* — R-0078, 48M, Northeast

### 3.4 Convenience Prioritizers — 16% of sample (n=200)

**Segment ID: `convenience_prioritizers`**

The smallest segment and the most difficult to activate on sustainability. Convenience Prioritizers are younger (mean age 31), urban-concentrated, and organize purchase decisions around `channel_preference` (fast delivery, subscription auto-replenishment) rather than product attributes. Mean `environmental_concern` is 2.4, the lowest of any segment. `digital_savviness` and `technology_adoption` scores are the highest of any segment.

Their `information_sources` are narrow and algorithmic: retailer apps (Amazon, Instacart), influencer recommendations, and push notifications. They are largely immune to shelf-based sustainability messaging because they do not shop at shelf. **Only 12% have paid a premium for sustainable packaging**, and **34% explicitly prefer single-use packaging** for delivery reliability and portion control reasons.

**Headline behaviors.** `purchase_frequency` for explicitly marketed sustainable products is "rarely" (57%) or "never" (28%). 41% have never read a packaging recyclability claim. This segment should be engaged through product design (sustainability baked into default options) rather than through persuasion.

## 5. Brand & Messaging Implications

**Tone** — Messaging should run **specific and evidentiary** rather than aspirational. Conviction Buyers tolerate aspirational language; every other segment degrades trust when exposed to it without supporting claims.

**Forbidden language** — Across all segments, the unqualified words *"green," "eco-friendly,"* and *"natural"* drove statistically significant declines in message credibility (p<0.01). *"100% sustainable"* drew the highest skepticism score (mean 1.9/5 credibility).

**Messaging guardrails** — Any sustainability claim should attach to a measurable, year-stamped metric (e.g., *"43% recycled content, independently verified by [certifier] in 2025"*) or a recognized third-party certification. Claims without either consistently underperformed.

**Voice parameters** — For Conviction Buyers: confident, values-forward, long-copy tolerable. For Pragmatic Parents: family-framed, price-aware, short-copy. For Skeptical Shoppers: technical, citation-heavy, deliberate. For Convenience Prioritizers: minimal copy; lead with functional benefits (shelf life, portion control) with sustainability as a second beat.

---

## 6. Recommendations

1. **Segment your investment.** Do not message uniformly. A single creative targeting "sustainability-minded shoppers" underperforms segment-specific creative by an estimated 31% in recall and 24% in claimed purchase intent (Meridian A/B panel, parallel study).

2. **Certify or don't claim.** With 58% of the market actively distrusting generic claims, a sustainability claim without certification is net-negative in credibility for the Skeptical and Pragmatic segments, who together represent 62% of the sample.

3. **Engineer for Convenience Prioritizers.** Persuasion will not move this 16%. Make the default SKU the sustainable SKU in delivery-heavy channels, and let it ride without claim-based messaging.

4. **Price-parity SKUs for Pragmatic Parents.** The single largest addressable gain is a price-parity sustainable option in family-sized formats. 52% of Pragmatic Parents report they would switch immediately at parity.

5. **Lead with certification bodies in retail media.** Third-party certification recall was the strongest single predictor of switching in the segmentation model (β=0.43, p<0.001). Paid retail media placements that foreground certification logos outperform brand-led claims.
